A Seasonal Plumbing Maintenance Calendar For Southwest Florida Homes

By the Fort Myers Express Plumbing team

Most plumbing maintenance advice is written for a climate with four distinct seasons, which doesn't map cleanly onto Southwest Florida's actual year. We don't have a winter that freezes pipes, but we do have a wet season, a dry season, hurricane season, and the seasonal population swing that empties out or fills up entire neighborhoods depending on the month. This calendar is built around what actually happens here, not a generic national template that assumes conditions we simply don't deal with in Lee County or anywhere else in Southwest Florida.

Early Dry Season (November-December)

As rainfall drops off, tree roots throughout Lee County start searching harder for moisture, and that's exactly when they're most likely to find their way into a cracked or joint-weakened sewer line. This is a sensible time for a camera inspection if you've had any history of slow drains or recurring clogs, since catching root intrusion early with hydro jetting is considerably cheaper than waiting for a full blockage. It's also when many seasonal residents are returning, which makes it a good time to check that everything held up over an empty summer.

Peak Dry Season (January-March)

With the snowbird population back in full and dry conditions continuing, this is a reasonable stretch to schedule non-urgent maintenance you've been putting off: a water heater flush, a look at a slow-draining fixture, or a softener service if you have one. Demand for plumbers tends to be higher in season, so booking non-emergency work with some lead time is easier than trying to get a same-week slot during a busy stretch.

Late Dry Season Into Early Wet Season (April-May)

This transition period is worth a quick check of your sump pump (if you have one) and any exterior drainage before the rainy season's heavier rainfall arrives. It's also a sensible time to have a septic tank inspected if you're due, so any issues get addressed before the ground saturates and complicates the work.

Hurricane Season (June-November)

This is the longest stretch on the calendar and the one that needs the most specific attention. Confirm your main water shutoff, exterior cleanouts, and any backflow preventer are in good order at the start of the season, then do a quick walk-around before any storm that's specifically forecast for your area. Our backflow testing service covers the compliance side of that check if your irrigation system needs annual certification.

Departure Season (Any Time You Leave For An Extended Period)

This one isn't tied to a specific month since it depends on your own schedule, but for homes that sit empty for weeks or months at a time, whether that's a seasonal resident heading north or a rental property between long-term tenants, shutting off the main supply and having someone check on the property periodically matters regardless of when it happens. Our leak detection service is worth considering if you come home to any sign that something happened while the house was empty.

Why A Generic Calendar Doesn't Work Here

A maintenance schedule written for a colder climate spends most of its attention on frozen pipe prevention, something that's essentially irrelevant in Fort Myers. Meanwhile, it usually says nothing about tree root cycles, septic drainfield saturation, or the storm-surge risk that defines an entire third of our calendar year. Following a generic schedule here means missing the maintenance windows that actually matter and worrying about ones that don't apply, which wastes both time and money on the wrong priorities entirely.

If you're not sure where your home stands against any of this, whether it's overdue for a camera inspection, due for septic service, or you're just not sure your shutoff valve still works, a general check-up visit can cover the basics in one trip rather than guessing.

How This Calendar Changes By Location

The broad strokes of this calendar apply across Lee County, but the specific risks shift depending on where you are. In Cape Coral, the wet-season transition is worth extra attention because of how the city's canal system and fill soil respond to saturated ground; shifting soil around canal-front lots is more of a June-through-November concern than a year-round one. In Lehigh Acres, where septic is the primary wastewater system for most homes rather than a supplement to city sewer, the April-May septic check matters more than it would somewhere fully on municipal sewer, since a failing drainfield doesn't have a backup system to fall back on. In Gateway's newer, HOA-managed communities, backflow testing tends to matter more than root intrusion, since the sewer infrastructure itself is modern PVC rather than aging cast iron.

Homeowner Tasks Versus Professional Tasks

Not everything on a seasonal calendar needs a plumber. Running water through drain traps, testing a sump pump, and doing a basic visual walk-around after a storm are all things most homeowners can do themselves without special tools. Camera inspections, septic pumping, backflow testing and certification, and any diagnosis of a suspected leak or root intrusion genuinely require professional equipment and, in the case of backflow certification, a licensed tester whose results get filed with the utility. Knowing which category a given task falls into helps you budget your own time appropriately instead of either calling for something you could check yourself or attempting a DIY fix on something that genuinely needs real diagnostic tools and training to do safely and correctly.

What Happens If You Skip A Season

Missing one seasonal check isn't usually catastrophic on its own. The risk compounds when checks get skipped repeatedly, year after year, because small issues that would have been caught early are instead given more time to develop into bigger ones. A sewer line with minor root intrusion that goes unchecked for one dry season is a minor issue; the same line left unchecked for three or four dry seasons in a row is a much more likely candidate for a full collapse rather than a simple jetting or spot repair, at considerably higher cost. The value of a seasonal calendar isn't any single check on its own, it's the cumulative effect of catching small problems consistently before they compound into large ones.

Keeping It Simple

You don't need a complicated spreadsheet or a reminder app full of notifications to get most of the value here. Picking two or three anchor points, the start of hurricane season, the return of peak season if you're a seasonal resident, and one general check sometime in the dry season, covers most of what actually matters most. The goal isn't perfect compliance with every line item on this list; it's building enough of a routine that plumbing problems get caught while they're still small and inexpensive to fix, rather than left to surprise you at the worst possible time of year.
