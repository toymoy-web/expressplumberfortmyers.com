5 Signs You Might Have A Slab Leak (And Why It's So Common Here)

By the Fort Myers Express Plumbing team

A slab leak, a leak in the water line running under your home's concrete foundation, is one of the more genuinely stressful plumbing problems to think about, mostly because it's invisible until it isn't. The good news is that slab leaks almost always give off real, detectable warning signs before they become a major structural concern. Knowing what those signs actually look like is the difference between catching one early and finding out the hard way.

Why This Is Such A Common Issue In Fort Myers

Nearly every home in this region sits on a slab foundation rather than a basement or crawlspace, which is standard for Florida construction but means the water supply lines running to your kitchen, bathrooms, and water heater are often routed directly through or under that concrete slab. Add in the area's naturally hard water, which accelerates corrosion in copper and galvanized supply lines over time, and slab leaks end up being a genuinely routine call for us rather than a rare occurrence.

Sign 1: A Warm Spot On The Floor

If a hot water line is the one that's leaking, warm water seeping into the slab can create a noticeably warm patch on your floor, sometimes in a spot with no obvious reason to be warm, like the middle of a hallway or living room floor with no vents nearby. This is one of the more distinctive slab leak signs precisely because it's hard to explain any other way.

Sign 2: A Water Bill That's Climbed Without An Obvious Reason

A slab leak is often a slow, continuous leak rather than a dramatic one, which means it can run for weeks before anyone notices standing water. In the meantime, it's steadily adding to your water usage. If your bill has crept up over a couple of billing cycles and you can't attribute it to anything specific, like a new appliance, more houseguests, or seasonal irrigation, a hidden leak is worth ruling out.

Sign 3: The Sound Of Running Water With Everything Off

This is one of the simplest checks you can do yourself. Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, then check your water meter. If it's still moving, or if you can hear a faint sound of running water somewhere in the house with nothing turned on, that's a real sign water is flowing somewhere it shouldn't be, and a slab leak is one of the more common culprits.

Sign 4: Cracks In Flooring Or Walls

A slab leak that's gone unaddressed for a while can cause the ground beneath the slab to shift or erode slightly as water saturates the soil underneath, which can lead to hairline cracks in tile, flooring, or nearby drywall. This is a later-stage sign rather than an early one, and it's a reasonable trigger to get a leak detection inspection scheduled promptly rather than waiting.

Sign 5: Mold, Mildew, Or A Musty Smell With No Obvious Source

Persistent moisture under or around a slab can eventually work its way to a place where you can smell it, even if you can't see any visible water. A musty odor that doesn't track back to an obvious cause, like a recently flooded bathroom or a forgotten wet towel, is worth taking seriously, especially combined with any of the other signs on this list.

What Happens During A Real Slab Leak Diagnosis

The good news about modern leak detection is that it doesn't require guessing or breaking up your floor to find a leak. Acoustic listening equipment and thermal imaging can pinpoint a leak's approximate location from above the slab, which means the actual repair work targets a specific, confirmed spot rather than an exploratory dig. This matters both for cost and for how much of your home ends up disturbed by the repair.

Is It Always A Full Slab Leak?

Not every warm spot or water bill increase turns out to be a slab leak specifically. Sometimes what looks like one is actually a leak in a more accessible location, like under a sink or behind a wall, that just happens to share some symptoms. This is exactly why a proper diagnosis matters before assuming the worst or the most expensive explanation. A technician confirming the actual source and severity of a leak, rather than guessing from symptoms alone, is the difference between an appropriately-scoped repair and either overpaying for unnecessary work or underestimating a genuinely serious problem.

What Not To Do

If you suspect a slab leak, resist the urge to start cutting into flooring or drywall yourself to try to find it. Without acoustic or thermal equipment, a DIY search is unlikely to actually locate the leak and will likely cause unnecessary damage in the process. It's also worth avoiding the assumption that a slab leak automatically means a full re-pipe of the house; many slab leaks are addressed with a targeted spot repair or a repiping of just the affected line, run through an accessible path instead of back through the slab, once the exact location is confirmed.

If any of these signs sound familiar, getting it checked sooner rather than later genuinely does make a difference, both for the cost of the eventual repair and for how much of your home gets disrupted along the way.

Why Waiting Makes It Worse

A slab leak doesn't fix itself, and the underlying problem, whether it's corrosion, a pinhole leak, or a fitting failure, generally only gets worse over time rather than better. What starts as a small, slow leak can widen as corrosion continues, increasing both the amount of water loss and the risk of the kind of foundation and structural issues that come with prolonged water exposure under a slab. There's also a financial dimension worth being honest about: a slow leak running continuously for weeks or months adds up on your water bill even before you factor in any repair cost, so the "wait and see" approach rarely actually saves money in the end.

How This Differs From A Regular Pipe Leak

A leak in an accessible pipe, under a sink or in an exposed section of plumbing, is inconvenient but straightforward: you can usually see or hear it, and a repair doesn't require accessing anything beneath the foundation. A slab leak is categorically different because the pipe itself is inaccessible without either locating it precisely first or committing to exploratory demolition. That's the entire reason acoustic and thermal detection technology exists and why it's worth using rather than skipping straight to a guess-and-dig approach that risks damaging more of your home than necessary.

Insurance And Slab Leaks

Whether a slab leak is covered by homeowner's insurance depends heavily on your specific policy and the cause of the leak. Sudden, accidental damage is sometimes covered, while gradual wear and corrosion, which describes most slab leaks, often is not, since standard policies typically treat that as a maintenance issue rather than a covered peril. It's worth reviewing your own policy's language on this specifically rather than assuming either way, and a documented professional diagnosis is useful either way if you do end up filing a claim.
