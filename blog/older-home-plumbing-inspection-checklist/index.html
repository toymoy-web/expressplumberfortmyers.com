Buying An Older Fort Myers Home? A Plumbing Inspection Checklist Before Closing

By the Fort Myers Express Plumbing team

A standard home inspection covers plumbing at a fairly general level: visible leaks, water pressure, and whether fixtures operate. For an older Fort Myers home, that general check often misses the specific, region-relevant issues that actually matter most. Here's what's worth confirming specifically before you close, beyond what a typical inspector's report will tell you.

Ask About Sewer Lateral Material And Age

If the home was built before the 1980s, there's a real possibility the sewer lateral is cast iron rather than PVC, and cast iron has a finite, predictable service life that a visual inspection alone can't confirm. A standard home inspector typically doesn't run a camera down the sewer line as part of a general inspection. If the seller or listing doesn't have documentation of a recent sewer camera inspection, requesting one, or having one done yourself before closing, can reveal root intrusion, cracking, or advanced corrosion that would otherwise surface as a surprise, expensive repair shortly after move-in.

Confirm Water Source And Related Infrastructure

Whether a property is on municipal water or a private well changes what you need to verify. For a well-supplied property, ask about the well's age, the pump type and its age, when the pressure tank was last serviced, and whether recent water testing results are available. For municipal supply, confirm whether the property has had any documented plumbing issues tied to the specific neighborhood's known infrastructure age, since certain older sections of Fort Myers and North Fort Myers carry a higher known risk of aging cast iron or galvanized supply lines.

Check For Polybutylene Pipe

Homes built primarily in the 1980s and into the 1990s sometimes have polybutylene supply piping, a gray plastic pipe material known for brittle failure at fittings as it ages, and it's specifically excluded or limited by some homeowner's insurance policies due to its failure history. If a home's age falls in that general construction window, it's worth specifically asking whether polybutylene is present, since it's not always obvious without opening a wall or checking exposed sections in a utility area.

A quick visual clue: polybutylene pipe is typically a dull gray color, about half an inch in diameter, and often stamped with "PB2110" along its length. If you see this in an exposed area like a garage or utility closet, that's worth flagging for a closer look.

Ask About Septic System History If Applicable

For properties on septic, particularly common in areas like Lehigh Acres, request the septic tank's pumping history, its estimated age, and ideally a recent inspection report covering the tank and drainfield condition. A septic system that hasn't been pumped in many years, or one with an aging or undersized drainfield relative to the home's actual water usage, can represent a significant near-term expense that a general home inspection often doesn't dig into specifically.

Water Heater Age And Condition

A water heater's manufacture date is typically encoded in its serial number, and checking it takes just a few minutes. If a tank unit is already past 8 to 10 years old, factor a near-term replacement into your budget planning regardless of whether it's currently functioning normally, since hard water conditions in this region tend to shorten actual working lifespan compared to the unit's generic rated range.

Look For Signs Of Past Water Damage

Water stains on ceilings, warped flooring, or a musty smell in specific areas can indicate a past leak that was patched cosmetically without the underlying plumbing issue being fully resolved. This is worth asking the seller about directly, and it's a reasonable area to have a plumber specifically evaluate if a standard inspector's report flags anything ambiguous in this category.

Backflow Preventer And Irrigation System Status

If the property has an irrigation system, confirm whether its backflow preventer is properly registered with the local utility and current on required testing. This isn't always transferred cleanly during a sale, and inheriting a lapsed compliance status is a common, avoidable surprise for new homeowners who assumed everything was in order simply because the system was already installed and working.

Putting Together A Realistic Budget

None of this is meant to talk you out of an older home; older Fort Myers neighborhoods have plenty of genuine appeal, and plenty of older homes have well-maintained plumbing systems. The goal is simply to go into a purchase with real information rather than assumptions, so any near-term plumbing expenses are a known, budgeted factor in your decision rather than an unwelcome surprise a few months after closing. A dedicated plumbing inspection, separate from the general home inspection, is a relatively small upfront cost that can meaningfully change how you approach negotiating a purchase price or planning your first year of homeownership.

How This Differs From Your Standard Home Inspection

A general home inspector's plumbing scope typically includes checking that fixtures operate, looking for visible leaks, and testing water pressure at a few points. This is genuinely valuable, but it's not the same as a plumbing-specific evaluation, which can include camera inspection of the sewer lateral, a closer look at supply line material throughout the house, and a more detailed assessment of water heater and septic condition than a general inspector's checklist typically covers. Neither type of inspection replaces the other; they cover different levels of depth on the same underlying systems, and relying on only one leaves real gaps in what you actually know about the property before you commit to buying it.

What A Seller Might Already Know But Not Volunteer

Sellers are generally required to disclose known material defects, but "known" is doing a lot of work in that requirement. A seller who's lived comfortably with an aging cast iron sewer line that hasn't yet caused a visible problem may genuinely not consider it something to disclose, even though a buyer would want to know. This isn't necessarily dishonesty; it's a difference in what each party considers relevant. Asking specific, direct questions, rather than relying solely on the standard disclosure form, tends to surface more useful information than a general disclosure process alone typically produces.

Negotiating Based On What You Find

If a pre-purchase plumbing inspection turns up a genuine issue, an aging sewer lateral, a water heater near end of life, or a septic system overdue for service, that information is useful leverage in negotiating either a price adjustment or a seller-funded repair before closing, depending on how your specific purchase agreement and local market conventions handle these situations. Even when negotiation isn't a realistic option, knowing about a likely near-term expense in advance still lets you plan and budget for it rather than being caught off guard.

Timing The Inspection Within Your Purchase Process

A dedicated plumbing inspection is best scheduled during your standard inspection contingency period, alongside or shortly after your general home inspection, so any findings can still factor into negotiation before you're contractually committed. Waiting until after closing to have a plumbing system properly evaluated removes your ability to negotiate based on what's found, turning what could have been a negotiating point into simply a repair bill you're now fully responsible for as the new owner.
