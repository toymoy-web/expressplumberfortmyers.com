A Hurricane Season Plumbing Checklist For Fort Myers Homeowners

By the Fort Myers Express Plumbing team

Hurricane season brings a specific, predictable set of plumbing stresses to Fort Myers homes, and most of them are preventable with a little preparation beforehand and a quick check afterward. This isn't about panic-buying supplies; it's a short, practical list of what actually matters for the plumbing side of storm prep, separate from the roof, windows, and generator planning most storm checklists focus on.

Before The Storm

A few plumbing-specific steps are worth doing once a storm is tracking toward Southwest Florida, ideally with enough lead time that you're not doing them in the rain.

  • Know where your main water shutoff is. If flooding reaches the level of your water meter or main line, being able to shut off supply quickly matters. This is also useful information for the storm's aftermath if a line gets damaged.
  • Clear debris from exterior cleanouts. Sewer cleanouts and any visible drainage points around the property should be clear of leaves, mulch, and loose material before heavy rain starts, so water and any backed-up sewage has somewhere predictable to go rather than pooling near the foundation.
  • Test your sump pump if you have one. A sump pump that hasn't run in months is not something you want to discover is broken during the storm itself.
  • Fill bathtubs with water if a boil-water notice or utility disruption seems likely. Stored water isn't a plumbing fix, but having it on hand for flushing toilets manually is a genuinely useful backup if water service is interrupted.

A note on backflow: if you have an irrigation system or pool fill line with a backflow preventer, storm surge and flooding are exactly the conditions that device is designed to protect against. Confirming it's in good working order before hurricane season, not during it, is the better time to check.

During Heavy Rain And Flooding

Once a storm is actively passing through, there's not much active plumbing work to do beyond staying aware of a few warning signs. Water backing up into a tub or shower drain during heavy rain, rather than draining normally, can indicate the municipal sewer system or your own line is under more pressure than it can handle. In that situation, minimizing water use in the house (fewer flushes, no laundry) reduces the load on a system that's already stressed.

Why This Matters More Here Than Elsewhere

Storm-season plumbing stress isn't a generic risk we're describing for the sake of a checklist. Older sections of Fort Myers and North Fort Myers run cast iron sewer laterals that are decades past a typical service life, and ground saturation from heavy rain is a genuine, recurring cause of misaligned joints and cracked sections in pipe that age. Homes in Cape Coral, built on fill soil around an extensive canal system, see a related but distinct problem: shifting ground during and after heavy rain events. Neither of these is a once-in-a-decade risk in Southwest Florida; it's a normal part of owning a home here, which is exactly why we're written up on it.

If Something Does Go Wrong

If a storm reveals real damage, sewage backup, a burst pipe, or flooding tied to a plumbing failure, that's a genuine emergency and worth calling about right away rather than waiting to see if it resolves on its own. If the situation involves standing water damage or needs extraction and drying rather than a plumbing repair itself, our water mitigation service is the separate line to call, since that's a different kind of response than a standard plumbing dispatch.

A pre-season check of your main shutoff, cleanouts, and any backflow devices takes very little time and can meaningfully reduce how much a bad storm actually costs you. If you'd rather have someone confirm all of it before hurricane season builds up, that's a quick visit worth scheduling.

Septic Systems And Storm Season

If your property is on a septic system rather than city sewer, hurricane season adds a specific risk worth understanding. Heavy, sustained rainfall raises the water table around a drainfield, and a saturated drainfield can't absorb wastewater the way it normally would. That backup doesn't always show up as an obvious sewage smell right away; sometimes the first sign is just slower drains throughout the house or a toilet that takes longer to clear than usual. If your tank is already close to due for pumping heading into storm season, getting it pumped beforehand gives the system more working capacity during the weeks when it needs it most. This is a particularly relevant point for homes in Lehigh Acres, where septic is the primary wastewater system for most properties rather than a backup to municipal sewer.

Generator Owners: A Gas Line Note

If you run a generator during power outages, whether portable or a permanently installed standby unit, hurricane season is a reasonable time to confirm the gas line supplying it (if it's gas-powered) was installed correctly and hasn't developed any wear at the connections. A gas line failure during a storm, when you're relying on the generator most, is a genuinely bad time to discover a problem. If you're considering adding a standby generator before next season, that's plumbing-adjacent work worth planning ahead of time rather than during an active storm watch.

What A Storm Doesn't Excuse

It's worth being clear about one thing: storm damage to plumbing is a real and common occurrence here, but not every plumbing problem that shows up during hurricane season is actually storm-related. A pre-existing slow leak, an aging water heater already near failure, or a sewer line with root intrusion that was slowly worsening for months can all pick the worst possible time to finally fail, coinciding with a storm without being caused by it. Understanding the difference matters for insurance purposes and for understanding what actually needs fixing. A honest assessment after the fact, rather than assuming everything is storm damage, leads to a better long-term outcome for the actual plumbing system.

Building A Simple Storm Season Routine

Most of what's covered here doesn't need to happen every single time a storm is forecast. A sensible approach is a more thorough check once at the start of hurricane season each year (confirming shutoffs, cleanouts, backflow devices, and septic status if applicable), followed by a quick walk-around before any storm that's specifically forecast to bring heavy rain or storm surge to your area. That two-tier approach keeps the workload manageable while still catching the things that actually matter before they become emergencies.
