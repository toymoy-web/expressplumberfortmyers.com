Well Water 101: What Lehigh Acres Homeowners Should Actually Know

By the Fort Myers Express Plumbing team

If you moved to Lehigh Acres from a home on municipal water, well water is probably the biggest adjustment your plumbing system had to make. It's not automatically worse or more dangerous, but it is genuinely different, and understanding those differences helps you take care of it properly instead of guessing or ignoring it until something fails.

Why Lehigh Acres Relies So Heavily On Wells

Unlike Cape Coral, which has an active utility expansion project gradually converting neighborhoods to city sewer and, in many cases, city water, Lehigh Acres has no comparable program reaching most of the town. A large share of properties here draw their water from private wells rather than a treated municipal supply, a reflection of how the community was platted and developed decades before centralized utilities caught up with its size. That's not likely to change soon for most existing homes, which makes understanding your well genuinely useful rather than optional information.

What's Actually In Well Water Here

Southwest Florida sits on a limestone aquifer, and well water drawn from that aquifer typically carries more dissolved minerals, calcium and magnesium especially, than treated municipal water that's gone through a filtration and treatment process before reaching your tap. Beyond general hardness, well water in this region can also carry:

  • Iron, which shows up as orange-brown staining on fixtures, sinks, and laundry, and can give water a metallic taste.
  • Sulfur (hydrogen sulfide), recognizable by a rotten-egg smell, particularly noticeable with hot water.
  • Sediment, fine particles that can cloud water and gradually wear on fixture internals and appliance components.
  • Higher mineral hardness than municipal supply, for the same limestone-aquifer reasons that affect the whole region, just more concentrated since it hasn't been through a treatment process.

Not every well has all of these, and the exact profile varies even between neighboring properties depending on well depth and the specific geology beneath a given lot. That's part of why testing matters more than assuming your situation matches a neighbor's.

A genuinely useful first step: if you've never had your well water tested, or it's been several years, a comprehensive test covering hardness, iron, sulfur, pH, and bacteria gives you an actual baseline instead of guessing based on symptoms alone.

The Well System Itself

Beyond water quality, a well relies on equipment that a municipal-supply home simply doesn't have: a submersible or jet pump, a pressure tank, and a pressure switch that cycles the pump on and off to maintain consistent water pressure throughout the house. Each of these is a mechanical component that can fail independently of anything related to the water quality itself. A pressure tank losing its air charge, a failing pressure switch, or a worn pump are all common, unrelated-to-water-quality reasons a well-supplied home might suddenly have weak pressure or a pump that won't stop cycling.

When Softening Isn't Enough

A standard water softener addresses hardness, calcium and magnesium, through ion exchange. It does not remove iron, sulfur, or sediment on its own, though some combination systems are designed to handle a limited amount of iron alongside softening. If your well water has a strong sulfur smell or visible iron staining, a softener alone likely won't resolve those specific issues, and you may need a dedicated iron filter, an aeration system for sulfur, or a sediment pre-filter ahead of or instead of a standard softener. This is exactly why testing before choosing equipment matters: buying a softener for a sulfur problem doesn't fix the sulfur problem.

Maintenance That's Easy To Forget

A well system isn't maintenance-free just because it's out of sight. A pressure tank's air charge should be checked periodically, since a tank that's lost its charge makes the pump cycle far more often than it should, shortening the pump's lifespan. If you have a water softener, it needs regular salt refills and periodic resin bed maintenance to keep working correctly. And unlike a municipal supply, which is tested regularly by the utility, private well water quality is entirely your own responsibility to monitor, which is part of why an annual or biannual water test is worth building into a normal home maintenance routine here rather than treating it as a one-time event.

What This Means If You're Buying A Home Here

If you're considering a home in Lehigh Acres that's on well water, a water test and a look at the well system's equipment (pump age, pressure tank condition) is worth adding to your due diligence alongside a standard home inspection, since well-related items aren't always covered in a general inspector's scope the way they would be for a home on standard city supply.

Well water isn't something to be anxious about, but it does reward a bit of actual understanding over assumption. Testing first, treating for what's actually there, and keeping an eye on the mechanical side of the system covers most of what matters for a well-supplied Lehigh Acres home.

A Note On Well Water Safety

Unlike municipal water, which is tested and treated to meet EPA drinking water standards before it reaches your tap, private well water carries no such guarantee unless you're testing and treating it yourself. This matters most for bacterial contamination, which a standard hardness or mineral test won't catch. If your well is near a septic drainfield, agricultural runoff, or was affected by flooding, a bacterial test is worth doing specifically, not just a general mineral panel. Most Florida county health departments offer bacterial well-water testing at low cost, and it's a reasonable thing to do annually regardless of whether you're noticing any symptoms, since bacterial contamination doesn't always change the taste, smell, or appearance of water.

What Treatment Actually Costs Versus What It Saves

It's fair to ask whether treating well water is worth the upfront investment. The honest answer depends on what your specific water actually contains and how it's affecting your household. Iron staining that ruins laundry and requires frequent fixture replacement has a real, ongoing cost even before you factor in a treatment system. Untreated hard water shortens water heater and appliance lifespan the same way it does on municipal supply, just often more severely given the typically higher mineral content in untreated well water. Weighing a treatment system's cost against your actual repair and replacement history, rather than against a generic national average, gives you a more honest picture of whether it pays for itself over time.

Working With What You Have

If a full treatment system isn't in the budget right now, there are smaller steps that still help: a sediment pre-filter is relatively inexpensive and protects fixtures and appliances from the most damaging particulate matter, and simply knowing your water's mineral content lets you make informed choices about which appliances and fixtures are worth spending more on for durability. Treatment doesn't have to be all-or-nothing to be worthwhile.
